Overview

Benchmarks and tests of creativity for large language models are now abundant: LLMs realize aspects of combinatorial and exploratory creativity, and they exceed human performance on several psychometric tests. But these evaluations leave out the mode cognitive science treats as the deepest — transformational creativity, the ability to radically modify an existing conceptual space, such as a scientific paradigm, a musical genre, or the plot of a short story.

TwistBench closes that gap. A literary plot twist is a transformation of a conceptual space: a late reveal that forces the reader to rebuild the model of the story they had been holding. We collect plot-twist stories written by famous human authors and compare them against stories written by 71 LLMs, scoring every story on surprise, coherence, realism, and reveal diversity.

The Benchmark

Writing a story supplies a conceptual space; the plot twist is a single discrete artifact that restructures it. TwistBench asks whether LLMs can write a diverse set of stories with surprising, coherent, and realistic plot twists.

Task and inference

71 LLMs from 19 providers, from small open-weight models (Llama-3.2-3B) to frontier systems (Claude Opus 4.8, GPT-5.5). Each is sampled at three temperatures (0.9, 1.0, 1.2) with ten samples each — up to 30 stories per model, averaged across temperatures — and constrained to 2,000–3,000 words. That range surrounds the ~2,500-word median of the human stories, which keeps story length from acting as a confound.

Task prompt

Write a complete short story (roughly 2,000–3,000 words). The story must contain a plot twist: a late reveal that makes the reader re-interpret earlier events in a new light, yet is consistent with everything that came before. In hindsight it should feel prepared, not arbitrary. Choose your own subject and characters. Do not announce or explain the twist. Write only the story, no title or commentary.

Scoring dimensions

Creative products are conventionally evaluated on surprise, novelty, and utility; for literary surprise specifically the standard dimensions are surprise, coherence, consistency, and divergence. Given a story T, three dimensions are assessed on Likert scales:

Surprise Surp(T) ∈ {1,…,5}

How drastically must one reinterpret earlier story elements after the plot twist?

Coherence Coh(T) ∈ {1,…,5}

After the plot twist, do earlier story elements still hold together? Is the story still logically consistent?

Realism Real(T) ∈ {1,…,5}

Does the plot twist involve impossible elements (real ghosts, magic, time travel, sentient AI, aliens) that violate the notion of literary “fair play”?

Each dimension is scored by a three-judge ensemble — Claude Sonnet 4, GPT-4o, and Gemini 2.5 Flash — aggregated per dimension by the median against a fixed rubric. The judges are disjoint from the 71 evaluated models, which mitigates self-preference bias in LLM-as-a-judge. Over all n = 2,035 stories, judge reliability ICC(2,k) is good-to-excellent on every dimension: realism 0.91, surprise 0.88, coherence 0.79.

Diversity

Diversity is a crucial aspect of creativity, and LLMs have a documented tendency to homogenize on open-ended tasks. For a set of n stories 𝒯 = {T1, …, Tn} and a map f : T → W from a story to a concise summary of its twist, diversity is the average embedding dissimilarity among those twists:

Div(𝒯):= 1n(n−1) ∑i ≠ j dcos( φ(f(Ti)), φ(f(Tj)) )

f is gpt-4o-mini prompted to summarize each story's twist in one sentence; φ is the text embedding model all-mpnet-base-v2.

Realism as a constraint, not a dimension

A twist can be made "surprising" trivially by revealing that none of it was real — that discards the conceptual space rather than restructuring it. Consistent with tests that reward novelty only when it is sufficiently appropriate, realism is therefore treated as a constraint: a story's surprise and coherence count only when the story is fully realistic, Real(T) = 5.

S★(Ti):= S(Ti) · 𝟙[Real(Ti) = 5], Coh★(Ti):= Coh(Ti) · 𝟙[Real(Ti) = 5]

The transformational creativity score for a model m is then the average z-score of its gated mean surprise, gated mean coherence, and diversity:

TC(𝒯):= 13 [ z(S★m) + z(C★m) + z(Divm) ]

      Findings

      1. Frontier LLMs still underperform expert humans

      Across the full pool, the expert-human gold set ranks first on the realism-gated composite. The gap is not driven by one weak facet in the models: strong LLMs match or beat humans on individual dimensions and lose on the combination. Elaborate prompting can close it for selected models — in-context regeneration lifts Sonnet-4.5 to a tie — but neither reasoning-effort scaling nor temperature raises the ceiling in general.

      2. Two failure modes: mode collapse and breaking the world model

      Mode collapse. Frontier models generate high-quality twists that are largely the same twist. Clustering the reveals shows dense single-cluster occupancy for the strongest models — Claude Opus-4.5 repeatedly lands on one grief-denial reveal.

      Breaking the world model. Other models earn surprise and coherence with twists that measurably depart from physical reality — time travel, ghosts, "it was a synthetic being all along." These are surprising and internally consistent, and they violate literary fair play, which is exactly what the realism gate is for.

      3. Reasoning traces are process-level homogeneous

      Reading the reasoning traces of thinking models shows the creative process is nearly identical across systems: models pick the twist first, then retrofit a plot that supports it. Nobody discovers the twist by writing forward into the story. Homogeneity of process is a plausible mechanism for homogeneity of output.
